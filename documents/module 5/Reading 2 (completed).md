**Reading 2 (completed): UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**

**What to look for as you read:**

- The core values UNESCO identifies - which align with, and which extend beyond, NIST's trustworthiness properties?

UNESCO names four values (human rights and dignity; environment and ecosystem flourishing; diversity and inclusiveness; peaceful, just, interconnected societies) unpacked by ten principles (¶¶9-47). Privacy, transparency, and accountability align with NIST; environment, equity, AI literacy, and multi-stakeholder participation extend beyond.

- The 'right to privacy' as a precondition for other rights - how does this differ from privacy as one property among several?

UNESCO calls privacy "a right essential to the protection of human dignity, human autonomy and human agency" (¶32) - a foundation other rights stand on, not one attribute to trade off. That demands lawful basis, consent, and protection across the whole data lifecycle (¶¶32-34).

- 'Multi-stakeholder participation' - what does it imply for university AI governance structures?

¶47 requires participation of governments, civil society, researchers, marginalized groups, and youth "throughout the AI system life cycle." For a university: students and front-line staff belong on AI governance bodies with real votes - affected groups help set the rules, not merely receive them.

- The equity and environmental justice dimensions - are these addressed in our institution's current AI guidance?

UNESCO requires reducing AI's environmental impact "including but not limited to its carbon footprint" (¶18) and making benefits "available and accessible to all" (¶28). U of A guidance addresses neither compute footprint nor benefit equity; the closest gesture is free university-provided AI tools.

**Guided reading questions:**

- **Q1:** Legally non-binding but adopted unanimously by 193 states - what does this imply for its authority relative to the EU AI Act and the NIST AI RMF?

**Answer:** Unanimous adoption gives UNESCO the widest legitimacy and weakest teeth: soft law that sets a global floor without enforcement. The EU AI Act binds with penalties only in its jurisdiction; NIST is voluntary. Authority and enforceability run in opposite directions.

- **Q2:** How does the rights-based framing change the governance questions a university must ask before deploying AI in student advising?

**Answer:** The first questions become rights questions, not failure-rate questions: lawful basis and consent for advising data; students told when AI informs their record, with explanation and human review (¶38); discrimination precluded (¶¶28-29); a human keeping final determination (¶¶35-36).

- **Q3:** Compare UNESCO's values with NIST's trustworthiness properties - what does each address that the other does not?

**Answer:** UNESCO adds what NIST lacks: environment, global equity, gender, culture, AI literacy, multi-stakeholder participation, explicit human determination. NIST specifies what UNESCO only implies: validity and reliability metrics, TEVV processes, security engineering, risk tolerance, documented organizational functions.

- **Q4:** What does 'meaningful' human oversight require in a university context - what mechanisms make oversight more than a formality?

**Answer:** A named human with authority, competence, and time to override; documented sign-off before consequential outputs take effect; audit trails with independent review; a student-facing appeal path; the standing ability to shut the system off. Responsibility never transfers to the system (¶¶35-36).
